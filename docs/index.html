Where the score with the document comes from

For every query, a search engine gives each document a score and shows the documents with the highest scores first. The score is SPLADE's estimate of how relevant a document is to the query, so it decides which documents you see and in what order.

score(query, document) = SPLADE(query) · SPLADE(document)

In practice, it is the sum over the terms that the query and the document share of query weight × document weight. Each band below is one of these products, drawn from the query word that produced the term to the document word that produced it.

How it works

SPLADE turns a text into a vector with one weight for each of the 30,522 entries of the BERT vocabulary:

wj = maxi log(1 + relu(logitij))

Read it from the inside out:

  1. logitij: for every position i of the text and every vocabulary entry j, the model gives a score of how well j fits at that position, given the whole text. This is where expansion comes from: at the position of iron, entries such as anemia or deficiency can score high even though they are not in the text.
  2. relu: relu(x) = max(0, x), so negative scores become 0. Only the entries the model has evidence for survive, which is why the vector is mostly zeros.
  3. log(1 + …): large values are compressed (1 becomes 0.69, 10 becomes 2.4), so a few terms cannot dominate all the others.
  4. maxi: each entry keeps its highest value over all positions. That value is its weight wj.

The highest is 1.6, at the position of iron: so wanemia = 1.6, and anemia comes from iron.

Because SPLADE keeps the maximum, we're able to understand where each term's weight comes from. This is exact, not a guess. It tells you which word the weight was taken from, but not why the network gave it that weight.

Before SPLADE reads a text, the tokenizer adds two special tokens: [CLS] at the start and [SEP] at the end. They are not words, and through attention they read every word of the text. SPLADE takes the maximum over all positions, these two included, and uses them for terms about the text as a whole. Point at the terms in the examples above to see how many come from [CLS] rather than from a word.

A shared term counts as written in both texts when it appears in both the query and the document. Otherwise it counts as added by SPLADE: at least one of the two texts does not contain it, and it matches only because SPLADE added it. Terms are entries of BERT's vocabulary, which splits long words into pieces: anemia, for example, becomes an and ##emia.
